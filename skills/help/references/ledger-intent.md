# What a ledger says it is for

A ledger can carry a short statement of its own purpose: what it exists to do, who it serves, and
roughly where it is heading. A few sentences, written by whoever owns the ledger, rewritten whenever
the answer changes.

It is the only thing on a ledger that describes the ledger rather than a decision.

## Why it exists

Two familiar things sit behind it.

The first is a **mission statement** — an organization's enduring reason to exist. A department's
ledger tends to read that way: *"harmonizing recruitment across the three regional offices."*

The second is closer, and it comes from the military: **commander's intent**. Before an operation, a
commander states the purpose and the intended end state, so that people who lose contact with
headquarters can still act sensibly on their own. The plan will not survive contact; the intent has to.

Memolok's version inverts why that is needed. A soldier states intent as insurance against *losing*
contact. An assistant working on your ledger has no contact to lose — every session starts from
nothing, with no memory of the last one, and the ledger is the only thing that carried over. So the
stated purpose is not a fallback for a bad day. It is the normal way anyone picking the ledger up gets
their bearings.

And unlike a military operation, it does not expire when the job is done. It persists, and it gets
rewritten in place, across the whole life of the ledger.

## It informs; it does not rule

This is the part that surprises people who expect it to behave like a policy.

A decision that has nothing to do with the stated purpose is recorded exactly like any other. No
justification, no exception process, nobody asking whether it fits. Two things are equally likely: the
purpose is out of date, or the decision is a legitimate case nobody thought to describe.

Compare that with a matter someone decided not to act on — that *does* require a recorded reason,
because declining to act is itself a decision. Diverging from a stated purpose requires nothing,
because the purpose was never a commitment in the first place.

If you find yourself explaining to the ledger why a decision was in scope, something has gone wrong
with how the purpose is being used.

## Why no decision ever points at it

Decision records freeze when you commit them, and they are allowed to reference facts from the almanac
because those freeze too — a fact is fixed in substance the moment it is admitted, and correcting one
means admitting a new fact, not rewriting the old.

A ledger's purpose is the opposite. It is meant to be rewritten, casually, whenever it drifts. So
nothing may rest on it. A record that leaned on the purpose would silently change meaning every time
somebody tidied up the wording — which is exactly the kind of quiet rewriting of history the whole
model exists to prevent.

There is a second reason, independent of the first. A decision record has to make sense on its own
terms: the need it addressed, the facts it cited, the options weighed, the reasoning that closed it.
The same way a law is read from its own text and the things it explicitly refers to, not from what the
people who passed it happen to think today. If you needed the ledger's *current* description of itself
to understand a decision from two years ago, the record would not be self-contained.

So the rule is simple: read it before you start something new, never point at it from something
finished.

## Writing one

Short. What it is for, who it serves, roughly where it is going. There is no test it has to pass and no
required structure — unlike the need at the head of a decision, which must be specific enough that
something could later prove it satisfied or not. A purpose is allowed to be directional: *"moving
toward one shared pipeline"* is fine, and would be a poor decision need.

Rewriting it costs nothing and breaks nothing, because nothing depends on it. A ledger with no stated
purpose is also perfectly normal — most start that way.
