# Deciding not to

A decision that concludes "no, we are not doing this" is a full decision. It is committed the same way,
carries the same reasoning, names the same accountable decider, and takes its place in the ledger
permanently.

It is not a failed draft, an abandoned attempt, or a record of something that did not happen.

## Why it deserves the ceremony

Consider what the alternatives are when a team explores a direction and concludes it is wrong.

They can leave it in limbo — no conclusion, no record, the discussion simply stops. Three months later
someone raises it again in good faith, because as far as the organization is concerned it was never
answered. The whole debate re-runs, occasionally reaching the opposite conclusion for no better reason
than that different people are in the room. That is the **zombie decision**, and it is one of the most
expensive habits an organization can have, precisely because each individual re-litigation looks
reasonable.

Or they can accept something weak just to close the topic, which is worse: now there is a commitment in
force that nobody believes in.

A committed rejection ends both. The topic is closed, the reasoning is on the record, and when it comes
back — it will — the answer is available along with the conditions under which it was reached. If those
conditions have changed, that is a new decision with a new commitment, which is exactly right. If they
have not, the conversation is over in a minute.

## In practice

Rejection deserves the same facilitation as acceptance. Closing with a committed *no* should be offered
as readily as a committed *yes* — not treated as the outcome you land on when the process fails.

It comes up more often than people expect, because only one of its shapes is the obvious one. A team
that explored every option and found none workable is the familiar case. But a proposal declined while
the work carries on in another direction is equally a rejection, and so is a commitment the
organization made and has now decided against honouring. Those two tend to get written up as an aside
inside whatever *is* being done — which buries them, because an aside cannot be found later by someone
asking whether this was ever settled.

The reasoning matters as much as it does anywhere else. *"Rejected"* on its own is nearly worthless; what
a future reader needs is what was considered, what ruled it out, and what would have to be different.

One asymmetry is worth knowing: a rejected decision was never in force, so nothing follows from it and
nothing can supersede it. It is a closed question, permanently answered as of the moment it was answered.

A sealed rejection is often more honest than indefinite deliberation, and always more honest than a weak
acceptance.
