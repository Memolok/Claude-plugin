# Learning from what actually happened

Not everything the world does in response to a decision was something you thought to predict. Memolok
distinguishes three kinds of observation, and the distinction is where most of the value hides.

## Three kinds of surprise

**Expected** — the observation assesses something you actually committed to, and records whether it held.
These are the known unknowns: you named the bet in advance, and now you know.

**Emergent** — something nobody could reasonably have predicted. You replaced the authentication system to
fix latency, and six months later support calls are down forty percent. That satisfies and violates
nothing, because you never considered that authentication latency might drive support volume at all.
Unknowable unknowns.

**Deducible** — something nobody predicted but somebody reasonably could have. The consequence was
foreseeable; it simply was not written down at commitment. Knowable unknowns, and usually the most
uncomfortable category to sit with.

## What a pattern of results means

One expectation that did not hold is a data point. Decisions are bets, and bets lose.

A run of them is something else. When most of what an organization commits to fails to materialize, the
signal is not about any individual decision — it points at systematic over-forecasting, a culture where
optimistic numbers are how proposals get approved, or a real gap between what leadership commits to and
what the organization can deliver. That belongs in a strategic review, not a sprint retrospective.

The emergent and deducible observations are the other half of the value, and they are the half people
throw away. A backlog of consequences your domain did not anticipate is case-study material about how your
particular corner of the world actually behaves. The deducible ones are more pointed still: each is
evidence about a specific blind spot in how your organization forecasts.

## One warning about how you use this

It is tempting to split the three apart — treat the expected results as a performance measure and keep the
other two as interesting anecdotes.

Do not. The moment stated expectations become the metric someone is judged on, people stop stating
expectations, or state only the ones they are sure of. You lose the tail rigor that made the whole thing
work, and you will not notice, because the records will look complete.
