# Notes that promise nothing

A scratchpad is a note on a ledger. Freeform text, no title, no category, no status. Write it,
rewrite it, throw it away, whenever you like.

It exists because everything else on a ledger is a commitment of some kind, and not everything worth
keeping is a commitment.

## The gap it fills

Before scratchpads, material that turned up mid-work had two places to go, and both meant something.

A **fact** in the almanac is an admission the ledger stands behind — *"legal requires seven-year
retention"* — and once admitted it is fixed. You cannot edit it; you correct it by admitting a new
one that says the old was wrong. That discipline is the point, because decisions get made on the
strength of it.

A **matter** is something raised for someone to look at. It carries no truth value and no urgency,
but it does carry an expectation: it sits in a queue, and eventually a person picks it up and decides
whether to act.

Now consider a vendor's quote you pasted in, a page you scraped, some rough figures from an afternoon
of digging. You are not asserting any of it. You are not asking anyone to do anything about it. You
just want it somewhere you will find it again.

Push it into the almanac and you have told the ledger to stand behind numbers you have not checked.
Push it into the queue and somebody will eventually pick up a note that was never a request. Neither
is a small mistake: the first puts unstable content where decisions draw their premises, and the
second means real work gets lost among things nobody meant to raise.

So there is a third place, and its whole character is that it promises nothing.

## Two questions

They settle almost every case.

**Does anyone expect something to happen because of this?** If yes, it is a matter.

**Would it be bad if this vanished?** If yes, it is not a note — a note is something you can afford
to lose.

When it is genuinely unclear, a note is the safer choice. It can be turned into a fact or a matter
later. A matter cannot be un-raised; matters are permanent, and the only way to close one you
registered by mistake is to record that you looked at it and decided not to act.

## Why nothing can cite one

This is the part people push back on, and it is the point of the whole thing.

A decision record freezes when you commit it. It is allowed to reference facts from the almanac
because those freeze too — the premises a decision rested on stay exactly as they were on the day it
was made. That is what makes it possible, years later, to ask what a decision actually knew.

A note is the opposite. It is *designed* to be rewritten and deleted. If a decision could rest on
one, then every casual edit to a note would silently change what a sealed decision appears to have
been reasoning from — and someone could quietly delete the ground a commitment was standing on.

You can see why the answer is not "just be careful". The freedom to rewrite a note without thinking
is the entire feature. It only survives if nothing can be resting on the note.

So the rule cuts cleanly: **if something in a note is load-bearing for a decision, it has to be
admitted as a fact first.** Not copied — admitted, in its own words, as something the ledger stands
behind. Which is a small amount of work, and exactly the work that distinguishes a figure you checked
from a figure you pasted.

## Promoting leaves no trail

When a note turns out to matter and you admit it as a fact, or raise it as a matter, the new thing is
written fresh. The ledger records no link back to the note.

That is deliberate, and it follows from the same reasoning. A provenance edge pointing at something
deletable is a broken edge waiting to happen, and an edge pointing at something *editable* is worse —
it would claim a fact came from a note whose text has since changed to say something else.

The practical consequence is worth knowing up front: after promotion, the fact stands entirely on its
own wording, and the note stays a note. If you want the connection remembered, put it in the fact's
own text.

## What it is not

It is not a document store, a wiki, or a filing system. There are no folders, no tags, no versions,
and no history of who changed what. You get the note as it is now, when it was made, when it last
changed, and everyone who has ever edited it.

Nothing reviews notes. Nothing reminds you about them. Nothing marks one stale. A note untouched for
a year is behaving exactly as intended — that is what a place for things that promise nothing means.

And notes belong to the ledger, not to you personally: anyone on the ledger can read, edit and delete
them.

## Deleting

Deleting a note deletes it. There is no recovery window and nothing to undo.

That sounds harsh next to the rest of Memolok, where nothing can be deleted at all. It is the same
principle seen from the other side: everything else is permanent because things depend on it, and a
note can be deleted precisely because nothing can.
