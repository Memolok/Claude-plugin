# Decision debt

Organizations have profound amnesia. Look at a strategic initiative, a major expenditure, or a policy
from two years ago and you can see exactly *what* was done — the vendor contract, the budget line, the
reorganized team. The *why* is gone. At best you find a sentence saying the vendor "was the best fit",
which tells you nothing about the world as it stood, which alternatives were actually examined, or how
that one won.

This happens because documentation records results, and **the result is not the decision**. The result is
what the decision produced: an email, a plan, and later a state of the world — a contract signed, a truck
bought, a stadium built. The decision came before all of it. It was an informed bet about the future, and
recording only the result erases the reasoning that made the bet.

## Why it compounds

If technical debt is the price you pay tomorrow for shortcuts taken today, **decision debt** is the price
you pay tomorrow for choices you never properly recorded — and it accrues interest the same way. An
unrecorded decision does not sit there neutrally. It turns into one of these:

- **Cargo-culting** — a team repeats a practice because "we have always done it this way", with no idea
  which constraint originally justified it. The ritual survives; the reasoning does not.
- **Paralysis** — people freeze because they can sense invisible fences, policies, commitments, risks, but
  nobody can point to where those fences were put up, or by whom.
- **Zombie decisions** — an issue that was never honestly closed resurfaces every planning cycle. Nobody
  remembers whether it was killed, deferred, or just forgotten, so it gets debated again. And again.
- **Decision decay** — the world moves underneath the organization and nobody can tell which decisions
  need revisiting as a result. Regulations shift, tax treatment changes, a supplier changes policy. Which
  of today's practices rested on the old version?

There is a subtler variant: **the founder's style**. In many successful organizations a large share of
decision logic is implicit — it is simply how the founder thinks and judges, absorbed by osmosis, never
written down. When they step back or the company scales, that implicit logic becomes a bottleneck. People
either stop deciding because they do not know the "right" mindset, or they decide against it without
knowing. Recording decisions converts that style into something the organization actually owns.

Technical-debt reviews get funded. Decision debt is the same kind of liability, and mostly invisible.
