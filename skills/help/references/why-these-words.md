# Why the words are these words

Memolok's vocabulary is chosen, not inherited, and most of it is doing work. Where a term looks
whimsical it is usually load-bearing; where it looks bureaucratic it is usually borrowed from a field
that solved the same problem first.

## The fish, and everything that swims with it

**The fish** names the shape of a decision. Draw one and you get the silhouette: narrow at the **head**,
where a single sharpened need sits; widening through the **belly**, where options and arguments open the
problem up; narrowing hard at the **waist**, where it converges into one commitment; then fanning out
again into the **tail** of expected consequences.

The shape is the point, which is why the anatomy words are used instead of neutral ones like "section".
Position carries meaning, and a badly-shaped record is visible as a shape: one that starts wide was never
scoped, one that converges twice is two decisions in a trench coat, one with no tail promised nothing and
can never be assessed.

**The wake** is what a fish leaves behind it in the water as it passes. Everything the world does in
response to a decision — the results, the surprises, the things nobody predicted — trails behind the
decision in exactly that way. The metaphor earns its place by carrying two further truths: you can only
see a wake *after* the thing has gone by, and you cannot change it by swimming the stretch again.

**Bait** is the informal name for a matter — the raw thing that attracts a decision, before anyone has
worked out whether it is worth biting. New decisions are said to be **spawned**, from bait or from the
wake of an earlier one.

## Words borrowed from fields that got there first

**Ledger** and **transaction** come from accounting and law, where a dated entry recording a commitment
is never quietly rewritten once later work depends on it. That is precisely the discipline decisions
need, and the borrowing is deliberate rather than decorative — `retractable-vs-anchored.md` follows the
analogy through.

**Anchored** is the moored sense: something has attached itself to this decision, so it can no longer be
moved without dragging them. **Retractable** is its opposite, a posting that can still be withdrawn
because nothing has tied on yet.

**Verdict** is judicial. It names the moment judgment is rendered and deliberation closes — not which
option won, which is a separate and much less interesting fact.

**Almanac** is a published book of facts about the world, reissued rather than corrected in place. World
facts behave exactly that way: you admit a new one that corrects the old, and the old stays.

**Intent**, in **Ledger Intent**, is the military sense — *commander's intent*, a concise statement of
purpose and end state issued so that people who cannot reach headquarters can still act coherently.
That is closer to the job than *mission* or *vision*, both of which name a document an organization
produces and files away. It was chosen over both for a second reason: a department's ledger tends to
read like a mission and a project's like a vision, and forcing practitioners to pick a label for
something they should just be able to write would be a tax with no return.

The borrowing is not exact, and the difference is the interesting part. A commander states intent as
insurance against *losing* contact with a chain of command that normally exists. An assistant reading
your ledger has no such contact to lose — every session begins with no memory of the previous one. So
intent here is not a contingency for a bad day; it is the ordinary way anyone arriving at the ledger
finds out what it is for. It also does not expire with an operation the way military intent does: it
persists, and gets rewritten in place, for as long as the ledger lives.

**t₀** and **tₙ** are ordinary mathematical convention. Using notation instead of "created date" is a
reminder that what is being marked is a *moment of agency*, and that the distance between the two is
itself worth looking at.

## Words chosen to stay neutral

**Matter** is the most deliberate choice in the vocabulary, and the easiest to underrate. The obvious
alternatives all pre-judge: an *issue* implies something is wrong, a *complaint* implies someone is
aggrieved, a *bug* implies a defect, a *request* implies someone wants something. Every one of those
smuggles a conclusion into the intake step, before anybody has analyzed anything.

A matter, in the ordinary legal and business sense, is simply a thing raised for consideration. It
carries no truth value and no emotional polarity, and that emptiness is the feature: it is the only
honest container for input whose nature nobody has established yet.

**Claim** is chosen over "requirement" or "goal" for a related reason. A claim is a statement that can be
true or false — and falsifiability is the property that makes a need worth writing down, because it is
what lets a later observation settle whether it was met.

## Words that are just descriptions

**Decision debt** is an explicit analogy with technical debt, aimed at readers who already fund reviews
for the one and not the other. **Zombie** and **phantom** decisions describe what they look like from
across the room. **Polluted premise** and **stale constraint** say what they are.

None of this vocabulary is required of a user. It exists so practitioners can be precise with each
other; people who just want to record a decision should get plain language and their own domain's words.
