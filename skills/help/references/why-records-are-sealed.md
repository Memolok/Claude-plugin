# Why a committed record is not edited

A decision's effects project forward only. Nothing learned afterwards can travel back and change what was
decided, or what was known when it was decided. That is trivially true, and inconvenient enough that
professional life is full of habits which quietly deny it.

Memolok marks the moment of commitment and freezes the record's substance there. Three things depend on
that freeze.

## It protects the decider

The reward-and-punishment dynamic around decisions is asymmetric. People are **rewarded** according to the
effects of their choices, but **judged** according to the information they had at the time and the quality
of the process they ran. Those are different standards applied to the same act.

Freezing the record at the moment of commitment is what keeps the second standard honest. It preserves
what was actually known, so that a decision which looked sound on the available evidence still reads as
sound after the world went the other way. Without it, every past decision is judged on information its
author never had — retroactive blame creep, and it makes people stop deciding.

## It makes course correction possible

Correcting course requires comparing the reasons you set the original heading against how the world has
changed since. If old records are quietly touched up to fit the current situation, that comparison is
gone. You cannot tell whether you were wrong, whether the world moved, or whether you simply changed your
mind — and those call for different responses.

## It makes learning possible

Learning from a bad decision means reconstructing why it looked good. Poor judgment? Missing data?
Available data nobody looked at? Pressure from someone senior? None of that survives in a record that has
been exposed to the future.

## The retroactive edit

When a decision turns out badly the reflex is to go back and improve the documentation: soften the
rationale, add a qualifier implying you saw the complication coming, adjust what you said you expected so
it lines up with what happened. People do this to their own memories automatically.

In a decision ledger, that is not documentation hygiene. It is destroying evidence — erasing the one thing
the record existed to preserve, which is the state of your knowledge at the moment you committed.

A well-reasoned decision that failed teaches more than one edited to look prescient. The first tells you
something true about the world: the information was incomplete, the assumption was wrong, the risk was
underestimated. The second is fiction, and it will be believed.

The value of a record is its honesty, not its correctness.
